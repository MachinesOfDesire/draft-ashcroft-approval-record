        An Approval-Act Record for Standing Authorization Grants
                   draft-ashcroft-approval-record-00

Abstract

   A standing authorization grant is an artifact that outlives the event
   that produced it.  Existing authentication-strength vocabulary (acr/
   amr) describes authentication events and travels on tokens.  Nothing
   carries a record of how a standing grant's approval was performed to
   the party verifying the grant, possibly days later and with no human
   present.  This document defines an approval-act record: evidence of
   the approval ceremony, bound to the exact grant issuance approved,
   and carried in-band with the grant it authorizes.  It defines one
   initial evidence type (WebAuthn assertions over a hash of the
   canonical grant), states precisely which of the record's claims a
   verifier can recompute, which rest on vendor attestation, and which
   remain attested by the operator, and provides a worked example with a
   failure matrix.

1.  Introduction

   The Monday/Thursday problem, stated once.  An operator approves a
   delegation on Monday behind a hardware authenticator.  The agent acts
   on Thursday with no operator present.  A token minted Thursday
   carries Thursday's acr: a service credential, or nothing.  The
   approval act that actually stood behind the authority is invisible to
   every verifier that matters, and it cannot be reconstructed after the

   fact.  If it is not captured when the grant is made, it is gone.

   The unattended case is the reason standing grants exist, so this
   document treats it as the main case.

1.1.  Requirements Language

   The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT",
   "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", "RECOMMENDED", "NOT RECOMMENDED", "MAY", and
   "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in
   BCP 14 [RFC2119] [RFC8174] when, and only when, they appear in all
   capitals, as shown here.

1.2.  Terminology

   *  Standing authorization grant (grant): a signed artifact delegating
      authority to an agent, valid across many later presentations.

   *  Operator: the party that runs the agent and issues or obtains the
      grant.

   *  Approver: the human whose approval the grant is meant to reflect.

   *  Approval act: the ceremony in which the approver approves one
      grant issuance.

   *  Approval record (record): the evidence of the approval act defined
      in Section 3.

   *  Verifier: any party that checks the grant and its record,
      typically a relying party receiving the agent's request.

   *  Operator anchor: the operator's JWK Set, published at the URI the
      Signature-Agent field names, under a name the operator controls,
      as used by the Web Bot Auth protocol
      [I-D.ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol].

   *  Covered issued-at: the issuance time carried inside the grant
      bytes covered by the record's challenge.

   *  Approval interval: the verifier-local interval defined in
      Section 5.1 within which the approval act provably occurred.

2.  Design Constraints

   C1.  In-band.  The record arrives inside, or inseparably with, the
   grant it describes, checkable at request time from retained material,
   with no token exchange the verifier cannot perform.



Ashcroft, et al.           Expires 8 May 2027                   [Page 3]

Internet-Draft             Approval-Act Record             November 2026


   C2.  Evidence relates to its issuer.  The document states, per claim,
   who issues the evidence and what a verifier can recompute versus must
   take on attestation.  It never implies the evidence outranks its
   issuer.

   C3.  Reuse existing vocabulary. acr/amr semantics (OIDC
   [OpenID.Core]), including the existing amr value "hwk" [RFC8176] and
   the EAP ACR values [OpenID.EAP] for phishing-resistant
   authentication, are reused, not reinvented.

   C4.  Verifier-required, not self-declared.  Whether a record is
   demanded, and at what strength, is the relying party's policy.  An
   asserted value a verifier cannot check is treated as absent.

   C5.  Parameters, not classes.  Timing and artifact binding are
   parameters of the record rather than new assurance classes; see
   Section 5.

3.  The Approval Record

   A grant carries at most one approval record.  The grant itself MUST
   carry a unique issuance identifier (a serial, or issuance timestamp
   plus id) as a covered field.  The REQUIRED form is deliberate.  An
   optional identifier would create a third state, bound but content-
   only, that every verifier handles differently and no vocabulary
   names.  A grant format that cannot carry an identifier is not
   excluded; it is priced, through captured binding, under Section 5.

   Fields of the record:

   *  evidence_type: registry identifier (Section 11).  This document
      defines "webauthn".

   *  evidence: the raw evidence artifact, carried inline.  For
      webauthn: authenticatorData, clientDataJSON and signature.

   *  attestation: OPTIONAL.  The authenticator's attestation statement
      for the approver credential.  Attestation belongs to the
      credential and is fixed at enrollment, so a verifier MAY retain it
      from the first record it sees for that credential, and a record
      MAY omit it when the verifier is expected to hold it already.  A
      record that omits attestation still carries the credential
      identifier, so a verifier can find the attestation it retained
      from an earlier record for that credential.  A verifier that holds
      no attestation for the credential, whether omitted, never sent or
      unobtainable, reports claim 2's hardware claim as unattested; it
      MUST NOT conclude invalid or unverifiable on that ground alone.




Ashcroft, et al.           Expires 8 May 2027                   [Page 4]

Internet-Draft             Approval-Act Record             November 2026


   *  credential: the approver credential ID and public key.

   *  binding: challenge = H(canonical grant bytes), where the grant
      bytes include the issuance identifier.  There is no record-side
      nonce.  The uniqueness that makes an approval unreplayable is a
      property of the grant, covered by the challenge, or it protects
      nothing.  Canonicalization is per grant format, not global: each
      grant format registry entry owns its canonical form.  The JSON
      grant format this document registers uses JCS [RFC8785]; a CBOR or
      binary format states its own.

   *  approved_at: timestamp of the approval act.  Operator-asserted;
      Section 5 states what that prices.

   *  parameters: artifact_binding and timing, per Section 5.

   The record, attestation included when present, MUST NOT exceed 8 KiB.
   A verifier that measures a record above the ceiling MUST conclude
   invalid: the check completed and the record failed it.  Unverifiable
   is reserved for a check that could not be performed.

3.1.  Carriage

   The record travels beside the grant.  Its challenge is the hash of
   the grant bytes, and a grant already signed by its issuer cannot take
   a new member without changing the bytes its issuer signed.  Keeping
   the record outside leaves the grant exactly as issued, so any grant
   format can carry a record, and no grant is re-issued to attach an
   approval.

   In JSON, the grant and its record travel together in a wrapper object
   with two members, grant and approval_record.

   Over HTTP, the record travels in its own header field, Approval-
   Record, alongside the grant.  When the request is signed under the
   Web Bot Auth protocol (HTTP Message Signatures [RFC9421]), the
   signature MUST cover the Approval-Record field.  A record stripped in
   transit then breaks the signature, where otherwise it would move the
   request silently from the record VALID column to record ABSENT
   (Section 8).  The 8 KiB ceiling keeps the field inside common default
   per-header limits.

4.  What the Record Claims, and Does Not

   Three claims, three issuers.






Ashcroft, et al.           Expires 8 May 2027                   [Page 5]

Internet-Draft             Approval-Act Record             November 2026


   Claim 1: This exact issuance was approved.  The verifier recomputes
   the hash of the grant it holds, issuance identifier included, and
   checks it against the signed challenge.  No issuer sits in that path;
   the claim is recomputable.  The choice of semantics is deliberate and
   stated: the approval binds an issuance, not a content.  Two re-issues
   of identical content are two approvals, and an approval of one is
   evidence for neither of the other.  This is what makes an old record
   unreplayable onto a re-issued grant.  A reader who wants content
   binding is asking for a different and weaker claim, and this document
   declines to blur them.

   Claim 2: The approval was performed on a hardware authenticator with
   a user present and verified.  The presence and verification flags
   ride inside the signed authenticator data, checkable against the
   credential public key.  The hardware claim rests on enrollment-time
   attestation.  Its issuer is the authenticator vendor: trust relocated
   to a party with no stake in any particular grant, not removed.  Where
   the verifier holds no attestation for the credential, the hardware
   claim is reported as unattested while the presence and verification
   flags still verify.  An authenticator identifier (AAGUID) carried
   without attestation is self-asserted and proves nothing about the
   hardware.

   Claim 3: That credential belongs to that operator's approver.  The
   residue.  The party being graded states it, nothing recomputes it,
   and an operator could enroll its own credential and manufacture
   human-looking approvals.  A verifier's confidence in claim 3 is
   exactly its confidence in the operator's enrollment practice, and
   this document says so plainly.

   The honest composite: when all three claims verify, some human
   holding the enrolled credential approved this exact issuance.

   Three partial states are named, so implementations have something to
   switch on other than a hard fail:

   *  APPROVED BY AN ENROLLED CREDENTIAL, HOLDER UNATTRIBUTED.  Claims 1
      and 2 verify; claim 3 cannot be evaluated (for example, the
      operator anchor is unreachable).

   *  APPROVED BY AN ENROLLED CREDENTIAL, HOLDER ATTRIBUTION UNRESOLVED
      AT REVOCATION.  Claims 1 and 2 verify, but the approver credential
      was revoked at a time inside the approval interval (Section 5), so
      the verifier cannot tell whether the credential was still held by
      the approver when the act occurred.  The name carries both facts
      on purpose.  A verifier needs to know which half is open, and a
      bare label would be implemented as a hard fail.




Ashcroft, et al.           Expires 8 May 2027                   [Page 6]

Internet-Draft             Approval-Act Record             November 2026


   *  APPROVED BY AN ENROLLED CREDENTIAL, HOLDER ATTRIBUTION EXPIRED.
      Claims 1 and 2 verify, but the operator anchor has lapsed
      (Section 8), so claim 3 rests on an expired anchor and the holder
      attribution expired with it.

5.  Parameters, Not Classes

   Two orthogonal parameters appear on every record.

   artifact_binding = bound | captured

   *  bound: the evidence's challenge is derived from the grant itself.
      Bound evidence is safely reusable across presentations of that
      grant precisely because it can attach to nothing else.

   *  captured: the evidence was produced at approval time but is not
      cryptographically bound to this grant.  Reuse is unsafe; verifiers
      SHOULD treat captured evidence as weaker than bound.

   timing = fresh | standing

   *  fresh: evidence produced within a policy-defined window of the
      presentation it accompanies.

   *  standing: evidence produced at grant time; the artifact outlives
      the ceremony.  This is the normal case for standing grants and the
      reason this document exists.

   A single class value cannot express that bound plus standing is safe
   while captured plus standing is not.  That asymmetry is the argument
   for parameters.

5.1.  Time, and Whose It Is

   approved_at has an issuer, and it is the operator.  Nothing the
   authenticator signs carries a clock. authenticatorData holds the RP
   ID hash, the flags, a signature counter (ordinal rather than
   temporal, and zero on a great many authenticators) and extension
   output. clientDataJSON holds the type, the challenge and the origin.
   So the record's one timestamp rides outside the signed material, and
   a verifier applying a freshness policy measured from approved_at is
   trusting the operator's clock while believing it is checking
   something.

   The situation is better than pure trust by exactly one interval.  The
   challenge covers the grant bytes, and the grant carries its issued-at
   inside those bytes.  The assertion cannot exist before the grant it
   covers, so the covered issued-at is a lower bound on the approval



Ashcroft, et al.           Expires 8 May 2027                   [Page 7]

Internet-Draft             Approval-Act Record             November 2026


   act.  The verifier's first sight of the record is an upper bound on
   its own clock.  The approval provably lies inside that interval, and
   approved_at is an operator-asserted point within it.

   Two freshness claims follow, named, so a verifier picks the one it
   can afford:

   *  ASSERTED freshness: a window measured from approved_at.  Operator
      grade: exactly as good as the operator's clock and honesty.

   *  CHECKABLE freshness: a window measured from the covered issued-at,
      evaluated against the verifier's own clock.  Recomputable from
      covered material, with no issuer in the path beyond the grant's
      own.

   Three rules keep the checkable claim checkable:

   1.  The upper bound is VERIFIER-LOCAL and MUST NOT be carried as a
       record field.  Two verifiers reading one record compute different
       intervals, and the narrower one is not more true.  Written into
       the record, the bound becomes one more operator-asserted
       timestamp under a new name.

   2.  First sight means FIRST USE.  The interval tightens only for a
       verifier that keeps state across uses of a standing grant.  A
       one-shot verifier gets the loosest interval, and one-shot is the
       common case.

   3.  The interval is only as independent as the grant's own issuer.
       Where the operator issues the grant, the covered issued-at is the
       operator's own assertion and the interval COLLAPSES to operator
       grade.  This caveat sits here, beside the interval, deliberately:
       a reader who takes the interval from one section and its collapse
       condition from another will implement the first and not the
       second.

5.2.  Revocation, Measured Against the Same Interval

   When the approver credential is revoked, the revocation's effective
   time is compared with the interval above, not with approved_at:

   *  Effective after the verifier's upper bound: the record stands; the
      credential was valid when the act occurred.

   *  Effective before the covered issued-at: invalid; the approver held
      no valid credential when the act is claimed to have happened.





Ashcroft, et al.           Expires 8 May 2027                   [Page 8]

Internet-Draft             Approval-Act Record             November 2026


   *  Effective inside the interval: genuinely doubtful, and doubtful
      verifier-locally in the same way the upper bound is.  Rules 1 to 3
      above govern it, and the result is the named partial state in
      Section 4, APPROVED BY AN ENROLLED CREDENTIAL, HOLDER ATTRIBUTION
      UNRESOLVED AT REVOCATION, which is not a hard fail.

6.  Evidence Type: WebAuthn

   Challenge construction. challenge = H(canonical grant bytes), where H
   is SHA-256 [RFC6234], fixed by this document.  There is no per-record
   hash field, because a negotiable hash is a downgrade lever every
   verifier must defend forever.  Agility lives in the registry: a
   future evidence_type, or a versioned entry, carries a different hash
   when one is needed.

   Verifier procedure.

   1.  Recompute H from the grant it holds.

   2.  Check equality with clientDataJSON.challenge.

   3.  Verify the assertion signature against credential [WebAuthn].

   4.  Read the UP and UV flags from authenticatorData.

   5.  Evaluate the attestation, from the record or retained from an
       earlier record for the same credential.  With none held, report
       the hardware claim as unattested.

   UV-required versus UP-sufficient maps to acr values per relying-party
   policy.

      [I-D.ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol]: supplies the operator

   *  draft-brezun-human-continuity-http
      [I-D.brezun-human-continuity-http], Section 7.5: the per-request

      values are unsatisfied unless the profile verifies evidence is the

      credential belongs to a unique human, and must say so explicitly.

   *  draft-morrison-acts-for [I-D.morrison-acts-for]: complementary.
      Acts-For defines a pass an agent carries when acting for a person
      and leaves the grant behind it out of scope.

   *  AAuth [AAuth] (Person/Mission): complementary.  The token path
      answers the interactive case; this document answers the standing
      case.

   *  Post-execution record layers: an approval record gives an
      execution record something to say about the question disputes
      actually litigate.  A record attributes; it never manufactures
      authority, and it cannot recover approval strength that was not
      captured at grant time.

8.  Worked Example and Failure Matrix

   One agent.  Operator anchor on one side (a JWK Set at a Signature-
   Agent URI the operator controls), approval-bound grant on the other.
   Materials are real WebAuthn assertions over real grant hashes,
   produced by a working prototype and labeled as such.  The prototype
   mints a JCS grant, runs the passkey ceremony over its hash and
   verifies per Sections 4 to 6.  The failure set is 13 vectors, one
   fault each, with each basis quoting the section it tests.  The
   current fixture is a 1Password passkey with attestation "none", so
   the verifier reports claim 2's hardware claim as unattested.

   Anchor outcomes are three: VALID; LAPSED, a conclusive outcome where
   the anchor expired and the verifier knows it; and UNREACHABLE, which
   is not an outcome because the check never completed.  Anchor changed-
   hands is deliberately absent from the matrix.  It is an ordering
   question between two events, which is record-layer territory,
   referenced rather than absorbed.

9.  Implementation Status

   Per [RFC7942], to be removed before publication as an RFC.  One
   prototype exists: a Cloudflare Worker that mints a JCS grant,
   performs the WebAuthn ceremony over its hash, verifies per Sections 4
   to 6, and generates the 13-vector failure set from a real ceremony.
   Coverage: one 1Password passkey fixture, attestation "none".  The
   vectors are available from the authors on request.

10.  Security Considerations

   *  Replay: an approval binds one issuance because the issuance
      identifier is inside the covered grant bytes.  There is no record-
      side nonce to manage and nothing to reuse across re-issues.

   *  Stolen approver credential: bounded by expiry and revocation of
      the credential enrollment.  Whether revocation casts doubt
      backward is decided by measuring its effective time against the
      approval interval (Section 5.2): after the interval the record
      stands, before it the record is invalid, inside it the result is
      the named partial state in Section 4.

   *  Self-enrollment (claim 3 residue): stated, not solved.
      Mitigations are possible at enrollment (attestation policy) and
      are out of scope here.

   *  Record stripping: a request whose signature does not cover the
      Approval-Record field can lose its record in transit and fall
      silently into the record ABSENT column; Section 3.1 requires
      coverage for that reason.

   *  Size ceiling: the 8 KiB ceiling keeps a record inside common
      default per-header limits on HTTP servers, and a record over the
      ceiling is invalid so that oversized records cannot be pushed into
      the unverifiable column.

   *  Grant revocation: revocation of the grant itself is unchanged by
      this document and must remain cheaper than approval.

   *  Credential correlation: the raw credential public key travels to
      every verifier of every grant the approver signs, and it is
      exactly the correlator the at-rest rule in Section 12 exists for.
      Pairwise keys stay incompatible with standing grants, so that
      rule, not the wire, is where correlation is limited.

12.  Privacy Considerations

   The record exposes an approval event and a credential identifier to
   every verifier of the grant.  Three facts follow.

   The credential public key is the sharpest exposure.  A verifier needs
   it to check the assertion, so the record cannot withhold it.  Where
   the approver is the person the agent acts for, every verifier
   receives the same key and can link all of that person's agents
   through it.  The raw key is exactly the correlator the at-rest rule
   below exists for.

   Pairwise credentials per relying party are INCOMPATIBLE with standing
   grants.  They require enumerating the verifiers at approval time, and
   the reason standing grants exist is that you cannot.

   The workable lever is at rest, not on the wire.  A verifier SHOULD
   store H(credential_id, per-verifier salt) rather than the raw
   identifier.  Per-verifier continuity survives, cross-verifier
   correlation ends, and C1 is untouched because nothing about the
   record changes.  The protocol cannot enforce this, since it is
   storage policy, which is why it is a verifier SHOULD rather than a
   wire mechanism.
